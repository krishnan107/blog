## The Problem with Current Tech Safety

It is June 23, 2026, and we are still having the same tired conversation about online safety. A new report released by the organization Chayn has finally put a finger on why digital abuse continues to spiral out of control. For years, tech giants and regulatory bodies have obsessed over a single metric: nudity. If an image contains exposed skin, the algorithms flag it and the moderators remove it. If it does not, the platforms often wash their hands of the situation.

But as many actresses and public figures are pointing out, this approach is fundamentally flawed. Focusing purely on nudity misses the human cost of digital violence. It ignores the fact that a photo does not need to be sexually explicit to be weaponized against someone. Stalking, harassment, and the non-consensual sharing of personal images are not just about what is being shown. They are about the violation of trust and the complete disregard for autonomy.

## Why Context Matters More Than Content

Think about the reality of modern online abuse. A person can be targeted with photos of their home, their children, or even altered images that place them in compromising situations without showing a single inch of bare skin. When tech companies prioritize nudity filters over behavioral patterns, they leave a massive gap for abusers to exploit. 

As the report highlights, the current framework is reactive rather than protective. We are chasing pixels instead of addressing the intent behind them. When an actress or any individual reports harassment, they are often told that their content does not violate community guidelines because it does not feature nudity. This is a cold and robotic response to a very human crisis. It tells victims that their pain is invalid as long as the images technically comply with basic safety policies.

## The Shift Toward Consent

We need a paradigm shift. We must move away from evaluating images based on content and toward evaluating them based on consent. If a person did not authorize the distribution of an image, it should be treated as a violation regardless of whether it is a photo of a dinner plate or a private intimate moment. Consent is the only standard that truly protects people. 

Tech companies have the resources to build better reporting tools, but they lack the will to change their fundamental business models. They prefer automated systems that check boxes rather than complex human systems that understand nuance. If we want the internet to be a safer place, we have to stop letting these companies hide behind flawed algorithms and start holding them accountable for the culture of harassment they facilitate.

It is time to demand more from the platforms we use every single day. We need to prioritize the dignity and safety of users over the convenience of automated moderation. Until we put consent at the center of the digital safety debate, we are simply putting a bandage on a broken system.
